PART 37

Reading the old books

There is no WPF book for .NET 9 or 10 and there probably won't be. The good news is that the two best books are about the parts that never changed.

WPF's core design (dependency properties, the visual and logical trees, layout, templates, binding) was settled long before these books were written and has hardly moved since. What changed is everything around it: how projects are built and deployed, how data is fetched, how view models are written. So an old book is reliable on the first set of topics and misleading on the second, and the sections below tell you which is which.

IN PLAIN TERMS

An old WPF book is like a workshop manual for an engine that is still in production in a car with a new dashboard. The chapters on how the engine works are as true as the day they were printed. The chapters on the radio, the key fob and the service schedule describe parts that have since been replaced, and following them will waste an afternoon.

What to read

WPF 4.5 Unleashed (Adam Nathan, 2013) is still the best explanation of how WPF thinks: dependency property precedence, the two trees, templating, the layout contract. Read it for Parts 3 through 10 of this document.

Pro WPF 4.5 in C# (Matthew MacDonald) is the reference-shaped complement: drier, broader, good when Nathan skips something.

Mastering Windows Presentation Foundation, 2nd ed. (Sheridan Yuen, 2020) is the only one written against .NET Core, but it builds its own MVVM framework from scratch rather than using the toolkit, which is exactly the work the toolkit now does for you.

Chapters to skip outright

Each item below names what an old book covers and, where there is one, what replaced it. Two terms first: XBAP (XAML browser application) was WPF running inside Internet Explorer, and ClickOnce is a Microsoft deployment technology that installs and updates an app from a web or network location.

  • Anything on XBAP, browser-hosted WPF, or partial trust. Removed.
  • ClickOnce chapters describing the .NET Framework publishing wizard. The mechanism survived; the tooling is different.
  • Theming chapters about Aero, Luna and Royale. Read Part 8 instead.
  • BackgroundWorker and Dispatcher.Invoke-heavy threading chapters. Read Part 17 instead.
  • WCF and ADO.NET data access. Use HttpClient, System.Text.Json, EF Core or Dapper (Part 33).
  • Hand-written INotifyPropertyChanged, RelayCommand and ViewModelBase. Read them once to understand the mechanics, then never write them again.
  • Properties.Settings and app.config. Read Part 20 and Part 34.
  • BinaryFormatter-based clipboard and drag-drop samples. Removed in .NET 9; .NET 10 added JSON-based clipboard methods instead, shared with WinForms.

Where to get the current picture

The books stop in 2013 for most topics and 2020 at best. These sources fill in the years since, and all of them are free.

  • Microsoft Learn, "What's new in WPF": one page per release from .NET 5 forward. Reading those short pages patches the entire gap.
  • dotnet/wpf on GitHub: the actual source, including generic.xaml with every default template.
  • WPF Gallery in the Microsoft Store: a live catalogue of what the Fluent theme currently styles.
  • CommunityToolkit/dotnet docs for the MVVM source generators.

Libraries worth knowing about

WPF ships deliberately small, and the community filled the gaps. None of these is required except the first, but knowing they exist saves you writing your own.

A reasonable order to learn in

If you're coming from ASP.NET Core or any other .NET background, the sequence that wastes the least time:

  1. Build one window by hand with a Grid, a TextBox and a Button, wired with code-behind. Twenty minutes. It makes the rest concrete.
  2. Read Part 5 on dependency properties until the precedence list is boring. Everything mysterious about WPF traces back to it.
  3. Read Part 11 on binding, then deliberately break bindings and watch the output window.
  4. Learn ContentControl + implicit DataTemplate (Parts 7 and 10). This is the architectural keystone.
  5. Adopt CommunityToolkit.Mvvm and the host (Parts 14 and 15) before you write your second screen, not after your tenth.
  6. Learn templating (Part 10) when you first want a control to look different, not before.

Step 4 is the keystone because it is how WPF apps switch screens: you put a view model into a ContentControl, and an implicit DataTemplate, one keyed by type rather than by name, picks the view that displays it. Step 6 is different: a control template replaces a control's whole appearance, which you rarely need early.

Styling and templating are where WPF's depth lives, and also where a week can vanish. Do them last, and do them in a dedicated resource dictionary rather than inline.

RECAP
  • Old WPF books are still right about the core: dependency properties, trees, layout, templates and binding.
  • Skip their chapters on XBAP, old themes, BackgroundWorker, WCF, app.config and BinaryFormatter.
  • The "What's new in WPF" pages on Microsoft Learn cover what changed since the books.
  • Use CommunityToolkit.Mvvm instead of writing your own MVVM plumbing, and install Snoop early.
  • Learn dependency properties and binding first, and leave styling and templating until last.
